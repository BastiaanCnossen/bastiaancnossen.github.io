Synthetic Category Theory · Chapter 3 · Section 3.5

Dependent products along exponentiable functors

← Previous chapterChapter 2 → Next chapterChapter 4 ← Previous sectionSection 3.4 → Next sectionSection 3.6

Recall from Section 1.9 the notion of a category over a base category \(S\). Given a functor \(f\colon S \to T\), base change along \(f\) provides an assignment \begin{align*} \{\text{categories}&\text{over $T$ }\} &&\to &\{\text{categories }&\text{over $S$}\} \\ p\colon C &\to T &&\mapsto &f^*(p)\colon C \times_T &S \to S. \end{align*} Semantically, we may think of this as the base change functor \(f^*\colon \Cat_{/T} \to \Cat_{/S}\), where \(\Cat\) denotes the universe of small categories (to be introduced in Chapter 8). This base change functor always admits a left adjoint \(f_!\colon \Cat_{/S} \to \Cat_{/T}\), given by postcomposition with \(f\): \begin{align*} \{\text{categories }&\text{over $S$ }\} &&\to &\{\text{categories }&\text{over $T$ }\} \\ q\colon D &\to S &&\mapsto &f \circ q\colon D &\to T. \end{align*} However, the functor \(f^*\colon \Cat_{/T} \to \Cat_{/S}\) will not in general admit a right adjoint; indeed, base change along \(f\) does not necessarily preserve all colimits. Let us illustrate this with the following simple counterexample:

Example 3.5.1. Consider the functor \(f = d_1\colon [1] \to [2]\) given by \(d_1(0) = 0\) and \(d_1(1) = 2\). We will argue that base change along \(f\) does not preserve pushouts of categories, and hence cannot admit a right adjoint. To see this, consider the following commutative square: We see as a consequence of the Segal axiom Axiom E that this is a pushout square of categories. If we pull back this square along the functor \(d_1\colon [1] \to [2]\), we obtain the commutative square But this is no longer a pushout square of categories, unless all categories are contractible. Indeed, if it were a pushout square, the two inclusions \(*\to *\sqcup *\) would extend to a functor \[ h\colon [1]\longrightarrow *\sqcup * \] whose values at \(0\) and \(1\) are identified with the two respective inclusions. Since \(*\sqcup *\) is an anima, \(h\) factors through \(\geom{[1]}\simeq *\), hence is identified with a constant functor. This means it takes the same values on \(0\) and \(1\), and we deduce that the two inclusions \(*\to *\sqcup *\) agree.

By the universal property of the coproduct, it follows that the identity functor of \(*\sqcup *\) is identified with the constant functor at either point. Thus \(*\sqcup *\) is contractible, which, by Lemma 1.6.18, implies that all categories are contractible. Consequently, the functor \(d_1\) fails to be exponentiable in every non-trivial model of our theory.

While this example means that \(f^*\) cannot admit a right adjoint for arbitrary \(f\), there is a large class of functors, called the exponentiable functors, for which this right adjoint does exist. This class includes cartesian and cocartesian fibrations, to be introduced in Chapter 4. In this case, we refer to the right adjoint as the dependent product along \(f\). We may already give its universal property right now in terms of animae of relative functors.

Definition 3.5.2 (Dependent product). Consider a functor \(f\colon S \to T\) and let \(p\colon C \to S\) be a category over \(S\). A dependent product of \(C\) along \(f\) is a category \(q\colon D \to T\) over \(T\) equipped with a functor \(\varepsilon\colon D \times_T S \to C\) over \(S\) which satisfies the property that for any other category \(E \to T\) over \(T\) the composite functor \[ \Map_{T}(E, D) \xrightarrow{f^*} \Map_{S}(E \times_T S, D \times_T S) \xrightarrow{\varepsilon \circ -} \Map_{S}(E \times_T S, C) \] is an equivalence.

If a dependent product of \(C\) along \(f\) exists, it is unique; see Exercise 3.8.6. We will denote it by \(f_*(p)\colon f_*(C) \to T\).

Terminology 3.5.3. Assume that \(C\) admits a dependent product \(f_*(C)\) along \(f\). Given a functor \(\varphi\colon D \to f_*(C)\) over \(T\), we obtain a functor \[ \varphi^u := \varepsilon \circ f^*(\varphi)\colon D \times_T S \to C \] over \(S\) that we will refer to as the relative uncurrying of \(\varphi\). Conversely, there is for every functor \(\psi\colon D \times_T S \to C\) over \(S\) a relatively curried functor \(\psi_c\colon D \to f_*(C)\), satisfying \[ (\varphi^u)_c = \varphi \qquadtext{ and } (\psi_c)^u = \psi. \] In this way, we may think of the equivalence in Definition 3.5.2 as a relative version of currying/uncurrying.

Construction 3.5.4. Consider a pullback square of categories, and let \(p\colon C \to S\) be a category over \(S\). Assume that:

  • • There exists a dependent product \(f_*(C) \to T\) of \(C\) along \(f\);

  • • There exists a dependent product \(f'_*(C \times_S S') \to T'\) of \(C \times_SS'\) along \(f'\).

We will construct a functor \[ \BC_*\colon f_*(C) \times_{T} T' \to f'_*(C \times_S S') \] over \(T'\) called the Beck-Chevalley map. By relative uncurrying, we may equivalently define \(\BC_*\) by specifying a map \((f_*(C) \times_{T} T') \times_{T'} S' \to C \times_S S'\), which we take to be the map \[ (f_*(C) \times_{T} T') \times_{T'} S' \simeq (f_*(C) \times_{T} S) \times_{S} S' \xrightarrow{\varepsilon \times_S S'} C \times_S S'. \]

We are now ready to introduce the class of exponentiable functors.

Definition 3.5.5. Let \(f\colon S \to T\) be a functor. We say that \(f\) is exponentiable if the following two conditions are satisfied:

  1. (1) For every pullback square and every category \(C'\) over \(S'\), there exists a dependent product \(f'_*(C') \to T'\) of \(C'\) along \(f'\).

  2. (2) For every pullback diagram and every \(p'\colon C' \to S'\), the Beck-Chevalley map \(\BC_*\colon f'_*(C') \times_{T'} T'' \to f''_*(C' \times_{S'} S'')\) is an equivalence.

Remark 3.5.6. Note that exponentiable functors are by definition closed under base change: if \(f\colon S \to T\) is exponentiable, then so is any base change \(f'\colon S' \to T'\) of \(f\).

Construction 3.5.7. Let \(f\colon S \to T\) be an exponentiable functor, let \(p\colon C\to S\) and \(q\colon D\to S\) be categories over \(S\), and write \(\varepsilon_C\colon f_*(C)\times_T S\to C\) and \(\varepsilon_D\colon f_*(D)\times_T S\to D\) for the evaluation functors. A functor \(\varphi\colon C\to D\) over \(S\) determines a composite \[ f_*(C)\times_T S\xrightarrow{\varepsilon_C}C\xrightarrow{\varphi}D \] over \(S\). Its relative currying is a functor \(f_*(\varphi)\colon f_*(C)\to f_*(D)\) over \(T\). The uniqueness part of relative currying supplies identifications \[ f_*(\id_C) = \id_{f_*(C)} \qquadtext{and}\qquad f_*(\psi\circ\varphi) = f_*(\psi)\circ f_*(\varphi). \] Thus dependent products are functorial in categories over \(S\).

Lemma 3.5.8. Let \(f\colon S \to T\) be an exponentiable functor. Then the assignment \(p \mapsto f_*(p)\) preserves pullbacks: given functors \(C_1 \to C_3 \leftarrow C_2\) over \(S\), there exists a preferred equivalence \[ f_*(C_1 \times_{C_3} C_2) \iso f_*(C_1) \times_{f_*(C_3)} f_*(C_2) \] over \(T\).

Proof

Functoriality of dependent products applied to the two projections constructs the displayed comparison functor. For every category \(E\) over \(T\), the universal properties of dependent products and pullbacks give equivalences \begin{align*} \Map_T\bigl(E,f_*(C_1\times_{C_3}C_2)\bigr) &\simeq \Map_S\bigl(E\times_T S,C_1\times_{C_3}C_2\bigr)\\ &\simeq \Map_S(E\times_T S,C_1) \times_{\Map_S(E\times_T S,C_3)} \Map_S(E\times_T S,C_2)\\ &\simeq \Map_T(E,f_*(C_1)) \times_{\Map_T(E,f_*(C_3))} \Map_T(E,f_*(C_2))\\ &\simeq \Map_T\bigl(E,f_*(C_1)\times_{f_*(C_3)}f_*(C_2)\bigr). \end{align*} These equivalences are induced by the comparison functor and are natural in \(E\). Taking \(E\) to be either side of the comparison constructs an inverse over \(T\) and identifications between the two composites and the respective identity functors, exactly as in the proof of Proposition 1.4.11. Hence the comparison is an equivalence.

Corollary 3.5.9. Let \(f\colon S \to T\) be an exponentiable functor. If \(p\colon C \to S\) is an embedding, then also \(f_*(p) \colon f_*(C) \to T\) is an embedding.

Proof

The identity functor of \(T\) exhibits \(T\to T\) as the dependent product of \(S\to S\) along \(f\), so there is a preferred equivalence \(f_*(S)\iso T\). Since \(p\) is an embedding, its diagonal square is a pullback. Applying Lemma 3.5.8 shows that the diagonal square of \(f_*(p)\colon f_*(C)\to f_*(S)\simeq T\) is a pullback. Hence \(f_*(p)\) is an embedding.

The terminology ‘exponentiable’ comes from the fact that it allows us to construct certain ‘exponential objects’ relative to a base category \(S\):

Definition 3.5.10. Let \(p\colon C \to S\) and \(q\colon D \to S\) be two functors and assume that \(p\) is an exponentiable functor. We define the relative functor category \(\ulFun_S(C,D) \to S\) as It comes equipped with an evaluation map \[ \ev\colon \ulFun_S(C,D) \times_S C \to D \] over \(S\) satisfying the property that for every other functor \(E \to S\) the composite functor \[ \Map_S(E,\ulFun_S(C,D)) \xrightarrow{- \times_S C} \Map_S(E \times_S C, \ulFun_S(C,D) \times_S C) \xrightarrow{\ev \circ -} \Map_S(E \times_S C, D). \] is an equivalence. In other words, \(\ulFun_S(C,D)\) is an ‘internal hom object’ or ‘exponential object’ for categories over \(S\).

Remark 3.5.11. Note that, for a category \(T\), the data of a functor \(T \to \ulFun_S(C,D)\) is equivalent to the data of a pair \((f,\varphi)\), where \(f\colon T \to S\) is a functor and \(\varphi\colon T \times_S C \to T \times_S D\) is a functor over \(T\).

Observation 3.5.12. For any functor \(f\colon T \to S\) and categories \(C\) and \(D\) over \(S\) such that \(C \to S\) is exponentiable, there is a pullback square In particular, the fiber of \(\ulFun_S(C,D) \to S\) over an object \(s\) of \(S\) is the functor category \(\Fun(C_s,D_s)\), where \(C_s\) and \(D_s\) are the fibers of \(C\) and \(D\) over \(s\), respectively.

Proposition 3.5.13. For every category \(S\), the terminal functor \(f_S\colon S\to *\) is exponentiable. Moreover, if \(p\colon C\to S\) is exponentiable and \(q\colon D\to S\) is any functor, there is a canonical equivalence \[ \Fun_S(C,D)\iso (f_S)_*\,\ulFun_S(C,D). \]

Proof

Let \(T\) be a category. The base change of \(f_S\) along \(T\to *\) is the projection \(\pr_T\colon T\times S\to T\). Given a category \(r\colon E\to T\times S\), let \(\delta_T\colon T\to\Fun(S,T\times S)\) be the currying of \(\id_{T\times S}\). Define \(\Pi_S(E)\to T\) by the pullback square Uncurrying the upper horizontal functor gives an evaluation functor \(\Pi_S(E)\times S\to E\) over \(T\times S\). For every category \(K\to T\), the pullback property and currying identify \[ \Map_T(K,\Pi_S(E)) \iso \Map_{T\times S}(K\times S,E). \] Thus \(\Pi_S(E)\) is a dependent product of \(E\) along \(\pr_T\). If \(T'\to T\) is a functor, the pullback defining \(\Pi_S(E)\) base changes to the analogous pullback over \(T'\), since functor categories preserve pullbacks. The resulting Beck–Chevalley map is therefore an equivalence. This proves that \(f_S\) is exponentiable.

Now let \(p\colon C\to S\) and \(q\colon D\to S\) be as in the statement. For every category \(K\), the universal properties of the dependent product and the relative functor category give equivalences \begin{align*} \Map\bigl(K,(f_S)_*\ulFun_S(C,D)\bigr) &\simeq\Map_S(K\times S,\ulFun_S(C,D))\\ &\simeq\Map_S(K\times C,D)\\ &\simeq\Map(K,\Fun_S(C,D)). \end{align*} These equivalences are induced by evaluation and are natural in \(K\). Hence the canonical functor \(\Fun_S(C,D)\to(f_S)_*\ulFun_S(C,D)\) is an equivalence by Proposition 1.4.11.

Corollary 3.5.14. Let \(p\colon C\to S\) be exponentiable and let \(q\colon D\to S\) be any functor. Then there is a canonical equivalence \[ \Map_S(C,D)\iso\bigl((f_S)_*\,\ulFun_S(C,D)\bigr)^\simeq. \]

Proof

The definition of \(\Map_S(C,D)\) and preservation of pullbacks by groupoid cores give \(\Map_S(C,D)\iso\Fun_S(C,D)^\simeq\). Now apply the preceding proposition.
