Synthetic Category Theory · Chapter 5 · Section 5.1

Categories in anima contexts

← Previous chapterChapter 4 → Next chapterChapter 6 ← Previous sectionSection 4.9 → Next sectionSection 5.2

Axiom K.1 (Categories in context). For every anima \(A\), there are categories, animae, functors, and identifications in context \(A\). These have the same primitives, operations, and specified coherences as the absolute theory.

We use the phrase absolute context for the theory considered so far. A category in context \(A\) may be denoted by \(B\), or by \(B(a)\) if we wish to display its dependence on \(a:A\). Similarly, we write \(f(a)\colon B(a)\to C(a)\) for a functor in context \(A\). When it is useful to display the context separately, we write \[ a:A\vdash B(a)\text{ category}, \qquad \qquad a:A\vdash f(a)\colon B(a)\to C(a). \] We denote the terminal category in this context by \(1_A\). The notations \(\Map_A(B,C)\) and \(\Fun_A(B,C)\) refer to the mapping anima and functor category formed in context \(A\).

Axiom K.2 (Axioms in context). Every axiom of the theory is available in an arbitrary anima context. This includes the rules of the present chapter and all future chapters.

Convention 5.1.1 (Iterated contexts). Since Axiom K.2 in particular applies to Axiom K.1, we may form iterated contexts: given an anima \(A\) in context \(\Gamma\), we may work in the further context \(\Gamma,a:A\). Thus a general context \(\Gamma\) may take the form of a finite string \((a_1:A_1, a_2:A_2(a_1), \dots, a_n:A_n(a_1,\dots,a_{n-1}))\). When such context \(\Gamma\) is fixed, we often leave it implicit in the notation; in this case the phrase “absolute context” refers to \(\Gamma\).

In light of Axiom K.2, an argument using the axioms introduced so far can be repeated in any anima context. For example, categories \(B(a)\) and \(C(a)\) have a product \(B(a)\times C(a)\) in context \(A\), with the projections and universal property of Axiom B.2. Similarly, the commutative-square, Segal, and Rezk axioms are available in each such context.

Axiom K.3 (Weakening). For every anima \(A\), there is a weakening operation \(W_A\) governed by the following rules. For an absolute category \(C\), we obtain a category \(W_AC\) in context \(A\), which is an anima whenever \(C\) is. For an absolute functor \(f\colon C\to D\), we obtain a functor \[ W_Af\colon W_AC\longrightarrow W_AD. \] For parallel absolute functors \(f,g\), there is an equivalence in context \(A\) of the form \[ \iota_{f,g}\colon W_A(f=g)\iso(W_Af=W_Ag)_A. \] For every primitive coherence in the theory, we require identifications relating the weakening of the absolute form of this coherence to its contextual form applied to the weakened inputs; see the explanation below. For each category constructor, the ‘canonical’ comparison functor in context \(A\) is an equivalence.

Convention 5.1.2 (Objects in context). When working in a fixed context, an object of a category \(C\) means an absolute object in that context, that is, a functor from its terminal category to \(C\). This also allows us to work with objects \(x\colon A\to C\) in the earlier sense: after passing to context \(a:A\), such a functor corresponds to an absolute object of \(W_AC\), as we will prove in Corollary 5.3.6.

Convention 5.1.3 (Omitting weakening). In context \(A\), we usually write \(C\) and \(f\) for the weakenings \(W_AC\) and \(W_Af\). Constructions involving these inputs are formed in context \(A\); their compatibility with weakening is understood as in Section 5.1.1. We write \(W_A\) explicitly when the distinction matters.

5.1.1 Preservation of specified structure

The weakening axiom, Axiom K.3, requires that the weakening operation is compatible with all postulated constructions of categories. For example, the weakening of a product is equivalent to the product of the weakenings, compatibly with the projections. It also requires that weakening preserves the specified identifications of the theory in the appropriate sense. The rest of this subsection spells out these requirements in more detail. On a first reading, one may continue with Section 5.2 and return here when a proof uses a particular compatibility.

The compatibilities we need will show up again in the discussion of substitution in Section 5.4. We will therefore explain them in the following abstract setting: We let \(S\) and \(T\) be two copies of the theory of synthetic categories, and describe the structure and rules determining a ‘change of theory’ \(W\) from \(S\) to \(T\). Weakening and substitution will then be instances of such change of theory.

Given a category \(C\) of \(S\), \(W\) determines a specified category \(WC\) of \(T\), which is an anima whenever \(C\) is. Similarly, a functor \(f\colon C \to D\) in \(S\) determines a functor \(Wf\colon WC \to WD\) of \(T\). We require specified identifications \[ W(\id_C)=\id_{WC}, \qquad W(gf)=(Wg)(Wf). \] For parallel functors \(f,g\colon C \to D\) in \(S\), we require an equivalence \(W(f =_S g) \iso Wf =_T Wg\); in particular, every identification \(\alpha\colon f = g\) in \(S\) produces an identification \(W\alpha \colon Wf = Wg\) in \(T\).

Consider now an expression \(X(F)\) in the abstract theory which depends on a tuple of input variables \(F = (f_1, \dots, f_n)\). We write \(X_S(F)\) for its interpretation in theory \(S\) and \(X_T(WF)\) for its interpretation in theory \(T\), applied to the tuple \(WF = (Wf_1, \dots, Wf_n)\). If \(X\) is a functor expression formed from variables using identities and composition, we recursively obtain an identification \[ X_W(F)\colon W(X_S(F)) = X_T(WF), \] as follows. If \(X(F) = f_i\) is a variable, we take this to be the identity identification. If \(X(f)\) is an identity or a composite, we take it to be the given specified identification. For an expression of the form \(Y\circ X\), it is defined as the composite identification \[ W(Y_SX_S) = (WY_S)(WX_S) \xrightarrow{Y_W\ast X_W}Y_TX_T. \]

Next, consider identifications \(\alpha\colon X = Y\) between two such expressions. Again this admits an interpretation \(\alpha_S\) in theory \(S\), and an interpretation \(\alpha_T\) in theory \(T\). In order to compare the two instantiations of this coherence, we must first ‘normalize’ \(\alpha_S\) to give it the correct source and target: we define \[ N_W^1(\alpha_S) \quad := \quad Y_W\cdot\bigl(W(\alpha_S)\cdot X_W^{-1}\bigr), \] which is an identification of the form \(N_W^1(\alpha_S)\colon X_T=Y_T\). Here we write \(\beta\cdot\alpha\) for the vertical composite of \(\alpha\) followed by \(\beta\). Now, for each primitive coherence \(\alpha\) of the abstract theory, we require \(W\) to come equipped with an identification \[ \alpha_W\colon N_W^1(\alpha_S) = \alpha_T. \] If \(\alpha\) is a more complicated identification expression formed by composition, inversion, or whiskering, we obtain an identification \(\alpha_W\colon N_W^1(\alpha_S) = \alpha_T\) by recursion on its structure in terms of those primitive expressions.

Finally, we consider identifications of the form \(p\colon \alpha = \beta\), with \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) both expressions in the abstract theory for identifications \(X = Y\). Generalizing the normalization procedure for \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) results in an identification \[ N_W^1(p_S)\colon N_W^1(\alpha_S) = N_W^1(\beta_S). \] By combining this with the already constructed identifications for \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), this in turn yields a ‘second-order normalization’ of the form \[ N_W^2(p_S) \quad := \quad \beta_W\cdot \bigl(N_W^1(p_S) \cdot \alpha_W^{-1}\bigr), \] which is now an identification of the form \(N_W^2(p_S) \colon \alpha_T = \beta_T\). We then require \(W\) to come equipped with identifications \[ p_W\colon N_W^2(p_S) = p_T \] for each primitive coherence \(p\colon \alpha = \beta\). The same rule applies at each further identification level present in the vocabulary: first compare the boundaries, and then compare the transported witness with the specified target witness.

Example 5.1.4 (The pentagon). Consider four composable functors \(f\), \(g\), \(h\) and \(k\), as in Axiom A.8. The two paths around the pentagon determine identification expressions \[ \alpha \quad := \quad \alpha_{g \circ f, h,k} \cdot \alpha_{f,g,k \circ h}, \qquadtext{ and } \beta \quad := \quad (\id_k \star \alpha_{f,g,h}) \cdot \alpha_{f, h \circ g,k} \cdot (\alpha_{g,h,k} \star \id_f), \] providing identifications between \(((k \circ h) \circ g) \circ f\) and \(k \circ (h \circ (g \circ f))\). If we instantiate these four functors in theory \(S\), there are now four ways to obtain an identification \(((Wk \circ Wh) \circ Wg) \circ Wf = Wk \circ (Wh \circ (Wg \circ Wf))\) in theory \(T\):

  • \(\alpha_T\) Instantiate \(\alpha\) directly in theory \(T\), applied to the tuple \(WF = (Wf, Wg, Wh, Wk)\);

  • \(\beta_T\) Instantiate \(\beta\) directly in theory \(T\), applied to \(WF\);

  • \(N^1_W(\alpha_S)\) Instantiate \(\alpha\) in theory \(S\), apply \(W\), and compose with the previously constructed identifications of its source and target \begin{align*} W(((k \circ h) \circ g) \circ f) &= ((Wk \circ Wh) \circ Wg) \circ Wf \\ W(k \circ (h \circ (g \circ f))) &= Wk \circ (Wh \circ (Wg \circ Wf)). \end{align*}

  • \(N^1_W(\beta_S)\) Instantiate \(\beta\) in theory \(S\), apply \(W\), and compose with the same two identifications.

Theories \(S\) and \(T\) provide pentagon identifications \(p_S\colon \alpha_S=\beta_S\) and \(p_T\colon \alpha_T=\beta_T\). The coherence data \(p_W\colon N_W^2(p_S)=p_T\) that \(W\) needs to provide can then be interpreted as requiring the following square to commute:

Let us now make precise the compatibility requirements of \(W\) with category constructors. In each case, we require a certain ‘canonical’ comparison functor to be an equivalence. For instance, the universal property of the terminal category \(1_T\) in theory \(T\) provides a functor \(W1_S \to 1_T\), which we require to be an equivalence. Similarly, the projections induce functors \(W\pr_1\colon W(C \times D) \to WC\) and \(W\pr_2\colon W(C \times D) \to WD\), and we require the induced functor \[ W(C\times D)\iso WC\times WD \] to be an equivalence.

The general procedure is as follows. Each category constructor \(X\) comes equipped with specified structure. We may instantiate this structure in theories \(S\) and \(T\), giving categories \(X_S\) and \(X_T\). The structure in \(S\) may be transported to \(T\) through \(W\). The universal property of \(X_T\) then provides a functor of the form \(W(X_S) \to X_T\) or \(X_T \to W(X_S)\), depending on \(X\), compatibly with the structure morphisms on both sides. We require this resulting functor to be an equivalence.

Remark 5.1.5. Some of the primitive coherences are functors on a product of identification animae. In order to express the compatibility of \(W\) with these coherences, we must combine the above normalization procedure with the equivalences \(W(C \times D) \iso WC \times WD\) and \(W(f = g) \iso Wf = Wg\).

If we have three instantiations \(S\), \(T\) and \(U\) of the abstract theory, and two changes of theory \(W\colon S\to T\) and \(V\colon T\to U\), we may combine them into another change of theory \(VW\colon S \to U\) by first using the compatibility for \(W\) and then the compatibility for \(V\): \[ VW(X_S)\xrightarrow{V_*(X_W)}V(X_T(WF)) \xrightarrow{X_V}X_U(VWF). \]
