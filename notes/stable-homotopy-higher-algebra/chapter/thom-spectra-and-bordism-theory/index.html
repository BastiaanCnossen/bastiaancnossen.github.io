Stable Homotopy Theory and Higher Algebra · Part I · Chapter 10

Thom spectra and bordism theory

← Previous chapterChapter 9 → Next chapterChapter 11 ← Previous sectionSection 9.4 → Next sectionSection 10.1

When is a closed smooth manifold the boundary of a compact smooth manifold? More generally, we may ask whether two closed smooth \(n\)-manifolds \(M\) and \(N\) are bordant, meaning that their disjoint union is the boundary of a compact smooth \((n+1)\)-manifold. In this chapter, we relate these geometric questions to stable homotopy theory through the construction of Thom spectra.

The foundations of bordism theory were laid by Thom (1954). Thom determined the unoriented bordism ring and characterized which closed smooth manifolds are boundaries in terms of their Stiefel–Whitney numbers. His work also addressed Steenrod’s problem of representing homology classes by maps from closed manifolds: every mod \(2\) homology class of a finite polyhedron can be represented in this way, whereas integral homology classes need not be representable by oriented manifolds.

The connection with homotopy theory builds on Pontryagin’s description of maps between spheres in terms of framed manifolds; see [Pontryagin (1959)] for an account. After stabilization, this identifies framed bordism groups with the stable stems \(\pi _*(\S )\). Thom’s construction allows one to dispense with the framings by replacing the spheres with the Thom spaces of universal vector bundles. As the rank varies, these Thom spaces assemble into the unoriented bordism spectrum \(\MO \).

Given a space \(X\), we may also equip our manifolds with maps to \(X\), producing the unoriented bordism groups \(\Omega _n^O(X)\). For a cell complex \(X\), the Pontryagin–Thom theorem (Theorem 10.3.6) gives a natural isomorphism \[ \Omega _n^O(X) \cong \pi _n\big (\MO \otimes \S [X]\big ). \] This identifies the bordism groups with the unreduced homology groups \(\MO _n(X)\) of the homology theory represented by \(\MO \). The systematic study of bordism and its associated cohomology theory was developed by Atiyah (1961a), working with oriented manifolds. Other choices of structure on the stable normal bundle similarly lead to bordism spectra, including the oriented bordism spectrum \(\MSO \) and the complex bordism spectrum \(\MU \).

To construct these spectra, we regard Thom spectra as twisted suspension spectra. For a rank \(n\) vector bundle \(E\to X\), fiberwise one-point compactification gives a family of pointed \(n\)-spheres indexed by \(\Pi _\infty (X)\), whose colimit describes the underlying anima of the Thom space under suitable hypotheses on \(X\); see Proposition 10.2.6. For a trivialized bundle, this is the constant family with value \(S^n\), and its colimit is \(\Sigma ^n(X_+)\). Passing to spectra leads to the definition of a Thom spectrum as the colimit of a family of sphere spectra, following Ando et al. (2014).

Stabilization also allows us to extend the construction to virtual vector bundles. Fiberwise one-point compactification carries direct sums to smash products, and after passing to spectra these become tensor products of invertible objects. Group completion therefore gives the stable J-homomorphism, which associates a family of sphere spectra to each virtual vector bundle. In particular, this construction gives the Thom spectrum of the negative tangent bundle, which we use in Atiyah duality in Section 11.3.

Finally, this point of view leads to a notion of orientation with respect to an arbitrary commutative ring spectrum \(R\). For a rank \(n\) bundle, an \(R\)-orientation identifies the associated family of sphere spectra, after tensoring with \(R\), with the constant family \(R[n]\). Taking colimits gives the Thom isomorphism; we recover its classical homological form after passing to homotopy groups. We develop this directly for virtual bundles in Section 10.4.

The compatibility of one-point compactification with direct sums also gives rise to multiplicative structures on bordism spectra. The technical proof that one-point compactification is coherently symmetric monoidal is postponed to Section 10.5, which may be skipped on a first reading.
