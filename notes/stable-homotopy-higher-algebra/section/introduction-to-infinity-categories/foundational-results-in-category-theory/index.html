Stable homotopy theory and higher algebra · Chapter 1 · Section 1.8

Foundational results in ∞-category theory

Having established the basic language and constructions of \(\infty \)-categories, we now collect a small amount of foundational infrastructure that will be used repeatedly later without further proof. Since our ultimate interest in this book is stable homotopy theory and higher algebra, we treat the results below as black boxes. Table 1.1 records additional background material developed later in the book.

The \(\infty \)-categories \(\Cat _\infty \) and \(\An \)

To formulate Yoneda-style constructions and presheaf categories, we need ambient \(\infty \)-categories of small animae and small \(\infty \)-categories. The following axiom isolates their key features that are used throughout this book.

Axiom L. There exist \(\infty \)-categories \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\), together with a fully faithful functor \(\An \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty }\). They satisfy the following properties:

  • There is a functor \(\pi _{\univ }\colon (\Cat _{\infty })_{\bullet } \to \Cat _{\infty }\) called the universal cocartesian fibration.1 Every object \(c \in \Cat _{\infty }\) defines an \(\infty \)-category \(C\) via the following pullback square:
    An \(\infty \)-category \(C\) arising this way is called a small \(\infty \)-category. If \(c\) is contained in the full subcategory \(\An \), then \(C\) is an anima; animae arising this way are called small animae.
  • For objects \(c,d \in \Cat _{\infty }\) corresponding to \(\infty \)-categories \(C\) and \(D\), there is an equivalence of animae \[ \Hom _{\Cat _{\infty }}(c,d) \iso \Map (C,D), \] and in particular morphisms in \(\Cat _{\infty }\) correspond to functors of \(\infty \)-categories. Composition in \(\Cat _{\infty }\) corresponds to composition of functors.
  • The \(\infty \)-categories \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\) admit all small limits and colimits; here the adjective ‘small’ means that the indexing \(\infty \)-category is required to be small. The inclusion \(\An \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty }\) preserves small limits and colimits.
  • The initial and terminal \(\infty \)-categories are small. Small \(\infty \)-categories are closed under products, coproducts, pullbacks, pushouts, functor categories, subcategories, and localizations.
  • There is a fully faithful functor \[ \simp \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty } \] given on objects by sending a linearly ordered set \(P\) to its associated \(\infty \)-category. In particular, the \(\infty \)-categories \([n]\) are small for all \(n \geq 0\).
  • There is a fully faithful functor \[ \Set \hookrightarrow \An \] given on objects by sending a set to its associated anima.

Remark 1.8.1 (Large \(\infty \)-categories). The \(\infty \)-categories \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\) are sometimes referred to as the universe of animae and the universe of \(\infty \)-categories. It will sometimes be convenient to assume there are two more such universes \(\widehat {\An }\) and \(\widehat {\Cat }_{\infty }\) that satisfy all the same conditions as \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\), with the following two further conditions:

  • There is a fully faithful inclusion \(\Cat _{\infty } \hookrightarrow \widehat {\Cat }_{\infty }\) which on small animae restricts to a functor \(\An \hookrightarrow \widehat {\An }\);
  • The \(\infty \)-categories \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\) are small with respect to \(\widehat {\Cat }_{\infty }\).

We will refer to \(\infty \)-categories that are small with respect to \(\widehat {\Cat }_{\infty }\) as large \(\infty \)-categories. The two points may thus be summarized by saying that every small \(\infty \)-category is also large, and that the \(\infty \)-categories \(\An \) and \(\Cat _{\infty }\) are large.

All categorical constructions are understood relative to fixed ambient universes, which we silently enlarge when necessary.

Remark 1.8.2 (Two meanings of ‘pullback square’). The ambient category \(\Cat _{\infty }\) lets us reconcile two pieces of terminology that we have so far used in parallel. In Section 1.3 we defined products, coproducts, pullbacks and pushouts of \(\infty \)-categories, together with the corresponding notions of pullback and pushout square, purely in terms of functors and natural isomorphisms. In Section 1.7 we defined limits and colimits inside an \(\infty \)-category, and hence in particular inside \(\Cat _{\infty }\). For small \(\infty \)-categories the two notions agree:

  • A commutative square of functors in the sense of Definition 1.2.2 between small \(\infty \)-categories is the same thing as a commutative square in \(\Cat _{\infty }\) in the sense of Definition 1.4.1. Indeed, by Axiom L the hom animae of \(\Cat _{\infty }\) are the mapping animae \(\Map (C,D)\), so filling a square of functors amounts to giving a natural isomorphism between the two composites.
  • Such a square is a pullback square of \(\infty \)-categories in the sense of Section 1.3 if and only if it is a pullback square in \(\Cat _{\infty }\) in the sense of Section 1.7, and similarly for pushouts, products and coproducts. For pullbacks and products, the first condition can be detected by bijectivity on \(\pi _0\) of the mapping animae out of every \(\infty \)-category \(T\), whereas the second is characterized by equivalences of these mapping animae. The former nevertheless suffices to conclude that the comparison functor is an equivalence. The statements for pushouts and coproducts are dual.

The one point to keep in mind is a matter of universes: the definitions in Section 1.3 quantify over all \(\infty \)-categories \(T\), whereas a (co)limit in \(\Cat _{\infty }\) only tests against small ones. By Remark 1.8.1 this is harmless, since the constructions of Section 1.3 are compatible with enlarging the ambient universe. From now on we use the two readings interchangeably.

Remark 1.8.3. In the quasicategorical model for \(\infty \)-categories, the statements in Axiom L are proved by [Lurie (2009)]. The quasicategory modeling \(\Cat _{\infty }\) is taken to be the homotopy coherent nerve of the Kan-enriched category of quasicategories, while the quasicategory modeling \(\An \) is the homotopy coherent nerve of the Kan-enriched category of Kan complexes.

Presheaves and free cocompletions

With these ambient categories in place, we can record the presheaf formalism that will be used repeatedly later.

Let \(C\) be an \(\infty \)-category. Then the hom animae \(\Hom _C(x,y)\) for \(x,y \in C\) assemble into a functor \[ \Hom _C\colon C\catop \times C \to \An \] called the Hom functor. Formally constructing this functor turns out to be surprisingly involved: it relies on the advanced concept of straightening/unstraightening from Chapter 23. We refer to Section 23.4 for a construction of the Hom functor.

Definition 1.8.4. A presheaf on an \(\infty \)-category \(C\) is a functor \(F\colon C\catop \to \An \). We define the presheaf category of \(C\) as \[ \PSh (C) \quad := \quad \Fun (C\catop ,\An ). \] By currying, the Hom functor defines a functor \(Y\colon C \to \PSh (C)\) called the Yoneda embedding; for \(x \in C\) we refer to the presheaf \(Y(x) := \Hom _{C}(-,x)\) as the presheaf represented by \(x\). A presheaf is called representable if it is isomorphic to \(Y(x)\) for some \(x\).

The \(\infty \)-categorical analogue of the Yoneda lemma is even more important than its classical analogue:

Theorem 1.8.5 (Yoneda lemma, [Land (2021), Propositions 4.2.10 and 4.2.11]). For a presheaf \(F\) on \(C\) and an object \(x \in C\), evaluation at \(\id _x \in \Hom _C(x,x) = Y(x)(x)\) induces an equivalence \[ \Nat (Y(x),F) \iso F(x), \qquad (\phi \colon Y(x) \to F) \mapsto \phi (\id _x). \] In particular the Yoneda embedding \(Y\colon C \hookrightarrow \Fun (C\catop ,\An )\) is fully faithful.

The following two results will be proved in Corollary 23.6.8 and Corollary 21.2.9, respectively.

Proposition 1.8.6. The functor \(\Hom _C(-,-)\colon C\catop \times C \to \An \) preserves limits in both variables separately.

Proposition 1.8.7. The presheaf category \(\PSh (C)\) admits all small limits and colimits. The evaluation functors \(\ev _x\colon \PSh (C) \to \An \) preserve all small limits and colimits.

Exercise 1.8.8. Let \(C\) be an \(\infty \)-category. Deduce that the Yoneda embedding \(Y\colon C \hookrightarrow \PSh (C)\) preserves all limits that exist in \(C\).

While the inclusion of \(C\) into \(\PSh (C)\) preserves all limits, it does not in general preserve colimits. Instead, it freely adds colimits to \(C\), in the following sense:

Theorem 1.8.9 (Lurie (2009), Theorem 5.1.5.6). Let \(C\) be a small \(\infty \)-category and let \(D\) be a large \(\infty \)-category admitting all small colimits. Then restriction along the Yoneda embedding \(Y\colon C \hookrightarrow \PSh (C)\) induces an equivalence \[ Y^*\colon \Fun ^{\colim }(\PSh (C),D) \quad \iso \quad \Fun (C,D), \] where the left-hand side denotes the full subcategory of colimit-preserving functors \(\PSh (C) \to D\).

More informally, this says that every functor \(u\colon C \to D\) admits a unique colimit-preserving extension \(Y_!(u)\colon \PSh (C) \to D\). This functor can be described directly in terms of the concept of left Kan extension, developed systematically in Section 21.4. Informally, a left Kan extension \(i_!(u)\colon E \to D\) of a functor \(u\colon C \to D\) along a functor \(i\colon C \to E\) is the best approximation to an extension of \(u\) to \(E\): its value at an object \(e\) of \(E\) is assembled as a colimit of the values \(u(c)\) over all maps \(i(c) \to e\). In the situation \(i = Y\) of the theorem, the full faithfulness of the Yoneda embedding guarantees that for \(e = Y(c)\), this Kan extension recovers \(u(c)\).

The special case \(C = *\) is the form that we will use most often in Part I. Since \(\PSh (*) \simeq \An \), it gives the following universal property.

Corollary 1.8.10 (Universal property of animae). Let \(D\) be a large \(\infty \)-category admitting all small colimits. Evaluation at \(* \in \An \) induces an equivalence \[ \ev _*\colon \Fun ^{\colim }(\An ,D) \quad \iso \quad D. \]

Given an object \(d \in D\), the inverse equivalence sends \(d\) to a functor of the form \[ \An \to D, \qquad X \mapsto \colim _X d, \] where the right-hand side denotes the colimit of the constant diagram with value \(d\), indexed by the anima \(X\).

Returning now to the simplicial language from Section 1.6, we may use this theorem to assign an \(\infty \)-category to every simplicial anima:

Construction 1.8.11 (Associated \(\infty \)-category). Every simplicial anima \(X\colon \simp \catop \to \An \) has an associated \(\infty \)-category \(\ac (X) \in \Cat _{\infty }\). To construct it, consider the inclusion \(\simp \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty }\) from Axiom L. By Theorem 1.8.9, this uniquely extends to a colimit-preserving functor \[ \ac \colon \sAn = \PSh (\simp ) \to \Cat _{\infty }. \] In particular, every simplicial set \(X\) (hence every quasicategory) defines an \(\infty \)-category \(\ac (X)\) by regarding it as a simplicial anima via the inclusion \(\sSet \hookrightarrow \sAn \).

Remark 1.8.12. Note that the composite \[ \Cat _1^{(1)} \xhookrightarrow {N} \sSet \hookrightarrow \sAn \xrightarrow {\ac } \Cat _{\infty } \] provides a way of associating an \(\infty \)-category to a classical 1-category. From this point forward, we stop working with Axiom B and use this refined assignment. The two assignments agree on the indexing categories used so far in this chapter: on the linear orders \([n]\) this is the fully faithful functor \(\simp \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty }\) from Axiom L, and the categories \([1] \times [1]\), \(\pushout \), \(\pullback \) and \([\omega ]\) are obtained from the \([n]\) by the coproducts and pushouts of Axiom D, which both assignments preserve.

Adjunctions

The last piece of black-box infrastructure we record here is the notion of an adjunction, which interacts closely with the limit and colimit constructions introduced above:

Definition 1.8.13. Let \(F\colon C \to D\) and \(G \colon D \to C\) be two functors. An adjunction between \(C\) and \(D\) consists of a natural isomorphism \[ \Hom _D(F(-),-) \cong \Hom _C(-,G(-)) \] of functors \(C\catop \times D \to \An \). We often write \(F \dashv G\) to indicate that such an isomorphism has been given.

As in classical category theory, adjunctions may equivalently be characterized in terms of a unit \(\eta \colon \id _C \to GF\) and a counit \(\epsilon \colon FG \to \id _D\) satisfying the triangle identities \((\epsilon F)(F\eta ) \cong \id _F\) and \((G\epsilon )(\eta G) \cong \id _G\). An important feature of adjunctions is that they can be characterized pointwise: given a functor \(F\colon C \to D\), if one can provide for every object \(Y\) of \(D\) a suitable object \(G(Y)\) of \(C\) equipped with a map \(\epsilon _Y\colon FG(Y) \to Y\) satisfying a suitable adjointness condition, then these objects automatically assemble into a right adjoint \(G\colon D \to C\), and the maps \(\epsilon _Y\) assemble into a counit \(\epsilon \) for the adjunction.

The theory of adjunctions is very rich and there is a lot to say about it. Details on the pointwise characterization of adjunctions, along with related concepts such as Kan extensions, (co)final functors, and Bousfield localizations, can be found in Chapter 21.

Example 1.8.14. Let us state as black boxes two fundamental examples of adjunctions we will use:

(1)

(Path components) The fully faithful inclusion \(\Set \hookrightarrow \An \) admits a left adjoint \(\pi _0\colon \An \to \Set \). This functor preserves arbitrary products.

We call \(\pi _0(X)\) the set of path components of the anima \(X\). We often abuse terminology and refer to an anima as a set if it is in the essential image of the inclusion \(\Set \hookrightarrow \An \) (cf. Example 1.1.4).

(2)

(Homotopy category) We say that an \(\infty \)-category \(C\) is a 1-category if the hom anima \(\Hom _C(X,Y)\) is a set for all \(X,Y \in C\). We denote by \[ \Cat _1 \hookrightarrow \Cat _{\infty } \] the full subcategory spanned by the 1-categories. Then this inclusion admits a left adjoint \[ \Ho \colon \Cat _{\infty } \to \Cat _1. \] Given an \(\infty \)-category \(C\), we refer to the 1-category \(\Ho (C)\) as its homotopy category; it has the same set of isomorphism classes of objects as \(C\), but has hom sets given by the set of path components of the hom anima of \(C\): \[ \Hom _{\Ho (C)}(X,Y) \cong \pi _0(\Hom _C(X,Y)). \]

Remark 1.8.15. The functor \(\Cat _1^{(1)} \to \Cat _{\infty }\) from Remark 1.8.12 lands in the full subcategory \(\Cat _1\) of \(1\)-categories. In particular, every classical 1-category defines a 1-category in the above sense. Conversely, every 1-category arises this way. We will use the phrase ‘classical 1-category’ if we wish to refer to the classical definition of categories in terms of sets of objects and morphisms.
